# Before publishing: read the prayer aloud

Use this on one new long-form draft before rendering. This is an editorial
review, not an upload gate or a script template. Review the actual narration,
not just the headings or description.

| Listen for | Revise if… |
| --- | --- |
| **The opening sounds like a real moment.** Can you say in one sentence what the listener brought to this prayer? | The draft could open any video on any channel. Replace the broad claim with one ordinary detail, without telling the listener how they feel. |
| **The verse shapes the request.** Point to a phrase from the reading, then to the petition it prompted. | You could swap the passage without changing the prayer. Keep the KJV quotation intact and rewrite the petition in your own words. |
| **The prayer is easy to join.** Read the transition into prayer out loud. Can you hear where the narration stops explaining and starts speaking to God? | The prayer feels buried under commentary, or the first petition takes several breaths to say. Bring the petition forward and give it one clear sentence. |
| **The blessing still fits if nothing changes today.** Read the ending as someone still facing the opening situation. | It sounds like a diagnosis, a deadline for God to act, or a test of faith. Turn the prediction back into a request or blessing. |

## Worked example: Psalm 121:2, a morning prayer

**Before:** “Whatever you’re going through today, remember that God is there
for you. Lord, bless everyone watching and help them with everything.”

**After (excerpt, not a full production script):**

> Before you open the message you’ve been putting off, you can ask for help.
>
> “My help cometh from the LORD, which made heaven and earth.”
>
> Lord, I don’t know what I’ll find when I read it. Help me take in what it
> says before I decide what it means. Give me patience if the answer isn’t
> what I hoped for.

A closing blessing could return to that moment: “May the Lord give you
patience for the next thing you have to read, and gentleness for the next
thing you have to say.” The excerpt illustrates the move from a real moment to
Scripture to prayer. Don’t reuse the message, wording, or rhythm as a stock
opening on other channels.

## After release

While reviewing the draft, note where direct prayer starts in the finished
video. Use that timestamp to look at retention around the transition in
[YouTube Studio](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415). Compare
similar-length prayers on the same channel and note the number of videos and
views. If data is sparse, call the result inconclusive. Retention tells you
whether viewers stayed; it does not tell you whether they felt comforted.
Treat voluntary viewer feedback as individual experience, not proof of impact.

## Applied review: The Midday Watch, Proverbs 15:1 (draft 0102)

The original queued draft had a strong subject: the words we choose under
pressure at work. Its opener surveyed several situations before reading the
verse, and its first scene invited prayer without yet giving the listener
words to pray. This excerpt shows the editorial approach now applied to the
queued narration.

> The reply is typed, but you haven’t sent it. Before you do, hear Proverbs
> fifteen, verse one: “A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words
> stir up anger.”
>
> Lord, You know why those words upset me. Help me say what is true without
> trying to wound the person who will read it. Give me the patience to take
> out the line I wrote just to get even.

Keep the draft’s distinction between gentleness and accepting injustice.
A later petition can ask for courage to speak clearly about what was wrong,
even if the other person does not respond well. For the closing blessing,
return to the unsent reply without promising reconciliation: “May the Lord
steady your words when it’s time to answer.”

**Before rendering:** Listen to the revised scene against the original opening.
Check whether the earlier verse and petition make the midday prayer easier to
join without losing the quiet pace. Use the revised script for both narration
and captions; do not reuse either from an older render.
