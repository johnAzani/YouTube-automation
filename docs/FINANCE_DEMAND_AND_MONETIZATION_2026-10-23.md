# Finance demand, watch time and money: what the public evidence actually shows

**Date:** 23 October 2026  
**Status:** research and decision memo. Not financial, legal, tax or investment advice. No publishing, rebrand, purchase or approval is authorized by this document.  
**Relationship to earlier work:** this note **revises the topic recommendation** in
[`FINANCE_NICHE_RESEARCH_2026-10-23.md`](FINANCE_NICHE_RESEARCH_2026-10-23.md). The earlier note correctly identified a production-fit format, but it did not have topic-level demand evidence. That evidence changes the packaging and the first three videos.

---

## The short answer

I checked the demand, the money and the watch-time question as far as public evidence allows.

**The finance category is huge. The specific "dry money mechanics" niche I previously recommended is not.** The category has hundreds of millions of monthly views, verified and reported creator earnings, and strong survey demand. But the demand is attached to **stakes, institutions, identity and people** — why rent is high, how banks profit, why you feel broke, how someone got out of debt. It is not attached to definitions and arithmetic. Top videos on "loan amortization explained" have roughly 200,000–340,000 lifetime views, often after 7–10 years. The exact query "credit card minimum payment trap" returned top results with **199, 337 and 22 views** — not thousands. That is not a huge-demand niche; it is a useful topic with almost no search pull.

**People are making insane money — but the documented cases are not "easy automation."** Joshua Mayo earned **$55,459.35 from ads in one month** at 269,000 subscribers in 2022, with a **$29.30 RPM** in that month. Caleb Hammer's company employs **28 people**, has about **73,000 paid YouTube memberships** and a budgeting app on track for **more than $3.2 million this year**. Those are real, reported numbers. They are also a personality-led business and a media company, not a passive faceless channel.

**Watch time is not public.** YouTube shows views, not watch hours. Only the channel owner sees average view duration and watch hours. Any "this channel gets X million watch hours" claim you see online is an estimate. I can show you the arithmetic: at a 6-minute average view duration, **40,000 views produce 4,000 watch hours**; at 15 minutes, only **16,000 views** are needed. The 4,000-hour threshold is not the hard part if retention is decent. Getting 1,000 subscribers and repeated discovery is harder.

**The revised recommendation:** build a **faceless, stakes-driven "money systems" explainer with original calculations** — not a channel about definitions, and not an AI finance guru. The calculation engine, charts and templates from the earlier plan stay. The topics and packaging change to the questions people already search for.

**No guarantee.** I will not tell you "everything will favour you." The honest version is: this is the best risk-adjusted bet I can find for your constraints, and it is testable for three videos, about 18 human-hours and no new spending.

## 1. What "huge demand" actually means

### 1.1 The category evidence is genuinely strong

The most directly relevant demand evidence I found is the **Precisify Insights: Finance 2026** report, based on a proprietary panel of **1,000 U.S. adults aged 18–55**, published 6 July 2026:

- **Almost one-third of U.S. adults watch finance-related content on YouTube every day.**
- Another **26%** watch two to three times per week.
- **More than half** prefer YouTube for financial "how-to" content, ahead of TikTok, podcasts, written articles, short-form video and email newsletters.
- Among main household earners, **72% have used YouTube to compare financial products or services**.
- YouTube is the most trusted listed source for financial information among main household earners at **17%**, ahead of financial advisers at **15%**, TikTok at **12%**, search engines at **11%**, finance influencers at **5%** and podcasts at **4%**.

That is not a niche hobby. It is a mainstream habit, and it sits close to product decisions — credit cards, accounts, insurance, investing. Advertisers pay for that proximity. [S1][S2]

YouTube's scale supports the same conclusion: roughly **2.7 billion monthly users**, **$40.37 billion in ad revenue in 2025**, and more than **20 million videos uploaded per day**. Finance is a small part of that ocean, but it is a valuable part. [S3]

### 1.2 What the survey does not prove

It does **not** prove that a new channel will get views. It is a U.S.-only, self-reported survey of 1,000 people, commissioned by a media-intelligence company that sells advertising technology. It tells us the audience exists and is accustomed to learning on YouTube. It does not tell us the supply of good channels, the click-through rate of your thumbnails, or whether YouTube will recommend you.

The category is also crowded. The same evidence that shows demand shows competition: multiple channels with 1–5 million subscribers, teams, and years of back catalogue. A new channel needs a reason to exist beyond "finance, but automated."

### 1.3 The distinction that matters

There are three different claims:

1. **Finance content has huge demand.** Supported.
2. **Finance content is highly monetizable.** Supported, with caveats about geography and format.
3. **A dry, easily automated money-mechanics channel will get huge demand and insane money.** Not supported by the topic-level evidence in Section 3.

The rest of this note is mostly about claim 3.

### 2.1 What jumps out

But it has a human creator, Darin Soat, who was anonymous until 2024; a distinctive voice; and a low cadence of roughly 0.8 videos per week over almost eight years. These are cautionary examples: quality and a back catalogue do not guarantee ongoing demand. [S12][S14][S15]

**High-authority finance can be smaller and still valuable.** Ben Felix has 645,000 subscribers and about 0.7–0.9 million views per month. That is not "insane" volume, but it is a highly qualified investing audience. The lesson is that there are two games: mass reach and high-value niche. They require different content.

### 2.2 The watch-time caveat

None of these tables show watch time. Public tools cannot see it. A 20-minute video with 1 million views may generate more watch hours than a 5-minute video with 3 million views. You can only compare formats, not channels, without private analytics.

### 3.3 The pattern is not subtle

**Definitions and arithmetic have a low ceiling.** The best "loan amortization" video has 337,000 views after ten years. The best "credit card interest" video has 294,000 after eleven years. These are useful, evergreen topics, but they are not mass-demand topics. The exact phrase "credit card minimum payment trap" returned top results with only **double- or triple-digit** views. That does not mean the subject is unimportant. It means people do not search for it in that form.

**Stakes, institutions and identity have a much higher ceiling.** "Why is rent so expensive" has 839,000 and 410,000 views on two recent videos, plus a 3.3-million-view Short. "How banks actually make money" has a 10-million-view Kurzgesagt video, a 473,000-view finance explainer, and two Shorts above 1.5 million. "Why you feel broke" has 852,000 views on one video and 204,000 on a four-week-old video. "Real cost of car ownership" has 472,000 views on a 20-minute video and 623,000 on a Short.

**Comparison content works.** "Debt snowball vs avalanche" has 840,000 views on an eight-year-old video and 856,000 on a Short. People want help choosing between two strategies; they do not necessarily want a lecture on how amortization works.

### 3.4 What this does to the earlier recommendation

The earlier note recommended "everyday money mechanics explained with original calculations," starting with borrowing costs and fees. The production logic was right: calculations are automatable, verifiable and visually clear. The **demand logic was wrong**. Starting with "here is how amortization works" would put the channel in the lowest-demand part of the category.

The correction is not to abandon calculations. It is to use calculations as the **proof inside a story people already care about**:

- Not "what is amortization" → **"Why paying the minimum keeps you poor."**
- Not "how do loans work" → **"The 72-month car loan that costs more than the car."**
- Not "what are fees" → **"The 1% fee that eats 20% of your retirement."**
- Not "how do banks work" → **"How banks make money from money they create."**
- Not "rent vs buy" → **"Why your rent keeps going up — and who profits."**

Same math. Different door.

## 4. Watch time: the number nobody publishes

### 4.1 What is public and what is private

YouTube publicly shows **views**, not watch hours. Watch hours, average view duration (AVD), average percentage viewed, impressions, click-through rate and returning viewers are visible only to the channel owner in YouTube Analytics. Third-party tools can estimate views and subscribers; they cannot see watch time.

That means every claim in the form "this channel gets 50 million watch hours" is either:

- the channel owner's private disclosure,
- a modeled estimate, or
- a guess.

There is no public dataset of finance-channel watch hours that I could find. I am not going to invent one.

### 4.2 The threshold arithmetic

YouTube's current long-form eligibility threshold is **1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 qualified public watch hours in the last 12 months** (or 1,000 subscribers plus 10 million qualified Shorts views in 90 days). From **1 February 2027**, new applicants face higher thresholds: **1,000 subscribers plus 8,000 qualified watch hours in 12 months, or 20 million qualified Shorts views in 90 days**. Existing YPP members are not affected by that particular change, but the terms still need to be accepted. Check the official page before applying. [S21][S22]

Four thousand hours is **240,000 minutes**. The number of views needed depends entirely on average view duration:

| Average view duration | Views needed for 4,000 hours | Watch hours per 1 million views |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 min | 80,000 | 50,000 |
| 4 min | 60,000 | 66,667 |
| 5 min | 48,000 | 83,333 |
| 6 min | 40,000 | 100,000 |
| 7 min | 34,286 | 116,667 |
| 8 min | 30,000 | 133,333 |
| 10 min | 24,000 | 166,667 |
| 12 min | 20,000 | 200,000 |
| 15 min | 16,000 | 250,000 |
| 22.5 min | 10,667 | 375,000 |

### 4.3 What finance benchmarks imply

Finance videos are long. One 2026 analysis of 1,503 finance videos put the **median length at 14.6 minutes**. Retention benchmarks for 10–15 minute videos cluster around **40–50%** for finance and business content. At 40–50% of 14.6 minutes, average view duration is roughly **5.8–7.3 minutes**. That implies **33,000–41,000 views** to reach 4,000 watch hours. [S23][S24][S25]

This is the most useful practical insight in the whole note:

> **The 4,000-hour threshold is not the hard part if retention is decent. A single video with 40,000 views at a 6-minute average view duration meets it. Getting 1,000 subscribers and repeat discovery is the harder, slower part.**

That does not make it easy. It means the plan should prioritise **retention and subscriber conversion**, not view-count vanity.

### 4.4 Why long-form still matters

Long-form accumulates more watch time per view. A 15-minute video at 45% retention produces **6.75 minutes per view**; 1 million views produce **112,500 watch hours**. A 60-second Short at 80% completion produces **0.8 minutes per view**; 1 million Shorts views produce only **13,333 watch hours** — and Shorts watch time does not count toward the 4,000-hour long-form threshold. Shorts are a discovery tool, not a substitute for long-form watch time. [S21]

This argues for a **hybrid**: 12–18 minute long-form explainers for watch time and ad revenue; 3–5 vertical cut-downs per long-form for discovery, not as the main revenue bet.

### 4.5 The honest limit

I cannot verify any competitor's watch time. What I can say is that the format with the most watch-time potential per view is the long-form documentary/explainer, and that the finance audience tolerates 14–18 minutes when the topic has stakes. The rest is our own analytics, once the channel is connected.

**Analytics gap:** the repository has `scripts/channel_analytics.py` and OAuth integration, but actual private authorization and report delivery are still unverified. Until that is connected, I cannot see your real watch time, AVD, CTR or RPM. Public research cannot substitute for your own channel data.

## 5. Money: verified, disclosed and estimated

### 5.1 The official definitions first

YouTube's own definitions matter because most "finance RPM" claims confuse them:

- **CPM** is what an advertiser pays per 1,000 ad impressions, before YouTube's revenue share. It counts only monetized impressions.
- **RPM** is total creator revenue after YouTube's share, per 1,000 **all** views — including views that had no ad. It includes ads, YouTube Premium, channel memberships, Super Chat and Super Stickers.
- RPM is therefore lower than CPM, sometimes much lower. A $20 CPM does not mean $20 per 1,000 views. [S26]

The standard long-form ad revenue split is **55% to the creator, 45% to YouTube**. For Shorts, the pool works differently. [S27]

These are the strongest numbers I found. "Verified" means a reputable outlet saw documentation; "reported" means a credible secondary source but not an audit.

US audience, 2022 ad market, personality-led. The $613,960 total is self-reported and includes non-ad revenue. [S28][S29] |

The pattern: **large money exists, but it is concentrated in people who built a business around the audience.** Ads are one leg. Memberships, apps, courses, sponsorships and affiliates are the others.

### 5.3 Why third-party "earnings" are mostly noise

Most "channel earnings" numbers you see are not revenue. They are models:

- **Social Blade** takes daily views ÷ 1,000 and multiplies by a fixed **$0.25–$4.00 RPM** range. It assumes every view is monetized. It is a scale indicator, not a paycheck. For finance channels, it systematically understates RPM; for channels with unmonetized views, it overstates.
- **HypeAuditor** reported The Ramsey Show Highlights at **82,900 average monthly views** in October 2026. **vidIQ** reported **34.88 million views in 28 days** for the same channel. Both cannot be right. The first is almost certainly a different metric or a bad model.
- **Nischa** is estimated at **$14,871/month** by vidIQ and **$1,595–$2,186/month** by HypeAuditor — a 7–10x spread.
- **Social Blade's own formula** is documented by third parties as a fixed $0.25–$4.00 RPM, not a finance-specific rate. [S33][S34]

The practical rule: **use public views to compare reach; never use a third-party earnings estimate to plan your life.**

**The geography multiplier is brutal.** Third-party estimates for Nigeria cluster around **$0.66–$2.50 RPM**, while U.S. finance audiences are estimated at **$8–$18** and creator-reported personal-finance RPMs cluster around **$10–$11** with outliers near $29. Nigeria is on YouTube's YPP availability list, so the country is not the blocker; the audience's viewing location is the revenue issue. [S35][S36][S37][S39][S40]

This is why "target a U.S. audience" is not greed; it is the difference between a hobby and a business. But it is also not a free lunch: U.S.-specific content requires U.S.-specific accuracy, and you are competing with U.S. creators who live inside the market.

### 5.5 Gross revenue is not profit

Caleb Hammer's operation has 28 employees. How Money Works has a human creator, an editor and a production workflow. Economics Explained is owned by a company with 11–50 employees. Even a solo channel has costs: research time, stock or original assets, music, captions, thumbnails, and the human hours you are not spending elsewhere.

At 4.2 million views per month and a $10 RPM, How Money Works would gross roughly **$42,000/month**. That sounds like a lot until you subtract a team, production and years of unpaid work. At a $6 RPM it is **$25,000/month**; at $15 it is **$63,000/month**. Those are plausible business-scale numbers, not "passive income."

### 5.6 The uncomfortable conclusion

**"People are making insane money" is true. "An easy automated finance channel will make insane money" is false.** The documented cases are:

- a personality with a 28-person company and a paid community (Caleb Hammer),
- a personality with a finance audience and sponsor/affiliate deals (Joshua Mayo),
- a personality with real-estate credibility and multiple businesses (Graham Stephan),
- team-owned explainer studios (Economics Explained, How Money Works).

The faceless solo operator can build a real business, but the evidence says it will be a **small media company**, not a lottery ticket. The realistic early milestone is 100,000 views/month, which pays **$600–$1,500** at $6–$15 RPM. That is the number to plan around, not the $30,000/month screenshots.

## 6. The niche I now recommend

### 6.1 The trade-off, in one table

|  | **Low demand** | **High demand** |
|---|---|---|
| **Easy to automate** | Dry definitions and arithmetic ("what is amortization") | Mass-produced listicles and AI slop — high output, low trust, policy risk |
| **Hard to automate** | High-authority niche analysis | Personality-led audits, roasts, debates (Caleb Hammer, Ramsey) |

The sweet spot is **medium automation, high demand**: a faceless, research-led **money-systems explainer** with original calculations. It is not as easy as definitions, and not as hard as being Caleb Hammer. It is the best overlap between your production advantages and the proven demand.

### 6.2 The positioning

**"The math behind the money story."**

Each video takes a question people already ask — why rent rises, why cars cost so much, why banks profit, why the minimum payment never ends — and answers it with:

1. a clear stake in the first 20 seconds,
2. an original calculation or simulation,
3. a visual proof (chart, waterfall, amortization table, fee stack),
4. the assumptions and sources,
5. one practical takeaway that is not personalised advice.

The differentiator is not "finance explained." That is crowded. The differentiator is **"show me the math, with the assumptions visible."** Many finance explainers assert; fewer show a reproducible calculation. That is a credible wedge for a small team, and it fits the repository's existing calculation and chart tooling.

### 6.3 Three content pillars

**Pillar A — The cost of ordinary decisions.** Cars, rent, leases, credit cards, buy-now-pay-later, subscriptions, insurance, fees. High stakes, high search intent, universally relatable.

**Pillar B — How institutions make money from you.** Banks, landlords, dealers, insurers, payment processors, fund managers. Systemic, shareable, and a natural home for original calculations. This is the How Money Works / Economics Explained lane, but with more explicit math and fewer vibes.

**Pillar C — The math of getting out.** Debt payoff, emergency funds, salary negotiation, refinancing, compounding. Positive, practical, and a natural bridge to future products (calculators, worksheets) without giving personalised advice.

### 6.5 What not to do

- **No AI finance guru persona.** YouTube's monetization policy says channels using AI-generated personas that present themselves as human experts giving advice on finances **will not be allowed to monetize**. A disclaimer does not fix a misleading persona. [S38]
- **No dry definition channel.** "What is amortization" is a segment, not a channel.
- **No high-volume, low-differentiation listicles.** Practical Wisdom is the warning: 1.03M subscribers, 1,466 videos, and about 186K views in 30 days in its last available snapshot.
- **No "make money online" pivot.** It can pay, but it is crowded, trust-sensitive and attracts policy and scam risk.
- **No stock/crypto signals or forecasts.** High regulatory and reputational risk; not a defensible niche for this studio.
- **No doomer misinformation.** How Money Works has been criticised in economics forums for confident, thinly sourced systemic claims. The wedge is the opposite: show the assumptions.

## 7. The honest outlook

### 7.1 Scenario table

Gross monthly ad/Premium revenue, before costs and tax:

| Monthly views | $6 RPM | $10 RPM | $15 RPM | $25 RPM |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 100K | $600 | $1,000 | $1,500 | $2,500 |
| 500K | $3,000 | $5,000 | $7,500 | $12,500 |
| 1M | $6,000 | $10,000 | $15,000 | $25,000 |
| 5M | $30,000 | $50,000 | $75,000 | $125,000 |
| 10M | $60,000 | $100,000 | $150,000 | $250,000 |

What the visible channels suggest:

- **100K views/month** is an early, plausible milestone for a new channel with a few good videos. It pays $600–$2,500 depending on RPM.
- **1M views/month** is a serious channel. It pays $6,000–$25,000 gross. This is the realistic "this could be a living" zone, not "insane money."
- **5M–10M views/month** is a team-scale operation. It pays $30,000–$250,000 gross, before 2–10 people, production and tax.
- **Caleb Hammer-scale (240M+ views/month)** is an outlier business with 28 employees, 5,000 videos and a paid community. It is not a template for a solo start.

### 7.2 What would need to be true for "insane money"

At least one of:

1. **Massive volume:** 50M+ views/month at a $5–$10 RPM, which implies a team and a high-cadence, high-retention format.
2. **High RPM plus scale:** 5M+ views/month at $20–$30 RPM, which implies a U.S./U.K. finance audience and strong advertiser intent.
3. **A product business:** memberships, an app, courses or tools layered on top of the audience, as Caleb Hammer did.
4. **Sponsorships/affiliates at scale:** finance sponsors pay well, but only after trust and audience size.

A solo faceless channel with 100K views/month and no product is none of these. It is a promising small business.

### 7.3 Why I will not give you a probability

There is no reliable public base rate for "new finance channel reaches 1,000 subscribers" or "reaches $10K/month." The data is survivorship-biased: we see the winners because they are visible. Anyone who gives you a percentage — "90% chance," "guaranteed within 6 months" — is guessing or selling something.

What I can do is make the bet **cheap, fast and reversible**, with explicit stop rules. That is the no-regret strategy.

### 7.4 The no-regret logic

- **No new spending.** Use existing tools, assets and time.
- **No job resignation.** Keep the optionality.
- **No rebrand.** Test the format before naming the company.
- **Transferable assets.** Every video produces a reusable calculation sheet, chart template, source pack and script structure. Even if the channel fails, those assets transfer to another channel, freelance work or a product.
- **Explicit stop rules.** You will know when to stop, rather than drifting for a year.

That is the closest thing to "I won't regret this" that an honest plan can offer.

## 8. Validation plan

### 8.1 Phase 0 — one week, no spend

1. Pick three topics from Section 6.4 (recommended: car cost, rent, banks).
2. Run the **demand gate** on each (below). If a topic fails, replace it; do not "push through."
3. Build a one-page source pack and a calculation sheet for each. Every number gets a date, a source and an assumption.
4. Write a 150-word hook and a thumbnail concept before writing the full script. If the hook is weak, the topic is wrong.

### 8.2 Phase 1 — three videos, six weeks, 18 human-hours

- **One video every two weeks**, not one per week. Quality and accuracy matter more than cadence at this stage.
- **Hard cap: 18 human-hours total** across research, script, calculation, visuals, captions and review. If a video exceeds 8 hours, simplify the format.
- **No new paid tools, stock subscriptions, voice actors or editors.**
- Human review and publishing remain with you. The repository's `human_rewrite` and `status: approved` flags stay untouched; `AUTO_PUBLISH` stays off.
- Publish public only after human sign-off. Private drafts are acceptable for the first cut.

### 8.3 The gates

**Demand gate (before scripting):** score the topic out of 12. A topic must score **9 or more** to proceed.

| Criterion | 0 points | 1 point | 2 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top long-form result | under 150K lifetime views | 150K–299K | 300K+ |
| Top-three long-form average | under 100K | 100K–299K | 300K+ |
| Recency | no result in 24 months | one result in 24 months | two or more results in 24 months |
| Stakes | definition or technical term | useful but dry | cost, institution, identity or comparison question |
| Competition | one channel dominates | two channels | three or more channels |
| Gap | existing videos are complete | some outdated framing | repeated unanswered questions or outdated numbers |

The three recommended topics score roughly 9–12. "What is amortization" scores about 7 because it is a definition with old results. "Credit card minimum payment trap" scores about 6 because the exact query has almost no views, even though the subject matters.

This is a screening heuristic from the snapshot, not a statistical law. If a topic scores below 9, drop it or reframe it around a stake.

**Production gate (during production):**

- ≤6 human-hours per video for the first three.
- ≤8 hours absolute ceiling. If exceeded, cut scope: 8–10 minutes, one chart, one calculation.
- No unpaid overtime binges. Burnout is a failure mode, not a badge.

**Audience gate (28 days after each video):**

- Median views across the three videos **≥2,000**, and
- average view duration **≥40%**, and
- click-through rate **≥4%** (YouTube Studio), and
- at least one video **≥10,000 views**, and
- comments show comprehension and trust, not just "AI slop" accusations.

These are decision heuristics. A single video can fail for reasons unrelated to the format; look at the pattern across three.

### 8.4 Phase 2 — six more videos, eight weeks

Only if Phase 1 passes:

- Six videos over eight weeks (roughly one every 10 days).
- Keep the 8-hour ceiling per video.
- Double down on the pillar that produced the best retention and subscriber conversion, not the one with the most views.
- Test one Short cut-down per video, but do not let Shorts consume the long-form time budget.

### 8.5 Continue / stop rules

| Signal after 3 videos | Decision |
|---|---|
| 2 of 3 pass the audience gate and production stays ≤6h/video | Continue to Phase 2 |
| 1 of 3 passes, production ≤6h/video | Iterate topics once; do not scale yet |
| 0 of 3 pass, but production is sustainable | Diagnose hooks/thumbnails/retention; one more topic iteration |
| 0 of 3 pass after two iterations | Stop or repackage; do not keep uploading on hope |
| Production >8h/video consistently | Stop and simplify the format before continuing |
| Accuracy/legal risk cannot be controlled | Stop the topic, not just the video |
| Comments show confusion or distrust | Fix clarity before chasing reach |

At the **end of Phase 1 (about week 7)**, decide whether to continue to Phase 2. At the **end of Phase 2 (about week 15)**, review:

- median views and watch hours,
- subscriber growth,
- average view duration and retention,
- click-through rate,
- comments and returning viewers,
- production hours per video,
- revenue (likely $0 before YPP),
- whether you still want to do this.

If you want a single "day 90" checkpoint, use it as an interim look during Phase 2, not as a final verdict. Six to nine videos are not enough to judge a channel's lifetime potential; they are enough to judge whether the format is worth another quarter.

### 8.6 What to measure

Once the channel analytics authorization is live:

- views, watch hours, average view duration, average percentage viewed,
- impressions and click-through rate,
- subscribers gained per video,
- traffic sources (search vs suggested vs Shorts),
- returning viewers,
- comments sentiment,
- RPM and revenue (after monetization),
- production hours and cash cost per video.

Until then, use public views only as a rough demand signal. Do not confuse views with watch time.

### 8.7 What would change this recommendation

- **A narrow topic outperforms the systems topics.** If "car cost" or "minimum payment" gets materially better retention than "banks" or "rent," narrow the channel around it. Follow the audience, not the original plan.
- **Production consistently exceeds 8 hours per video.** Simplify to 8–10 minutes, one chart and one calculation. Do not let the format collapse under its own ambition.
- **The audience is overwhelmingly Nigerian and RPM stays near $1.** The ad model will not produce the income you want. Consider brand deals, a paid calculator/tool, or a different audience strategy — not more of the same.
- **YouTube rejects the channel for inauthenticity.** Audit the scripts, visuals and narration for originality; do not resubmit the same thing.
- **The numbers cannot be made trustworthy.** If a topic requires data you cannot source or verify, drop it. A missing video is cheaper than a correction.

### 8.8 The analytics gap, stated plainly

The repository has analytics code and documentation, but the private YouTube authorization and actual report delivery are still unverified. I cannot see your real watch time, retention or RPM. The public research in this note is the best that can be done without that access. Connecting the channel is the single highest-value next step for measurement — not more third-party estimates.

## Appendix A — calculations

### A.1 The worked loan example

A $10,000 loan at a **12% nominal annual rate**, compounded monthly (1% per month), with equal payments at the end of each month and no fees:

| Term | Monthly payment | Total paid | Total interest |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 36 months | $332.14 | $11,957.15 | $1,957.15 |
| 60 months | $222.44 | $13,346.67 | $3,346.67 |

The longer loan lowers the monthly payment by about **$109.70**, but adds about **$1,389.52** in interest under these assumptions. Rounded final payments may differ by a few cents. This is an illustration, not a loan offer or advice.

### A.2 Watch-time arithmetic

`watch hours = views × average view duration in minutes ÷ 60`

Examples:

- 40,000 views × 6 min ÷ 60 = **4,000 hours**
- 1,000,000 views × 6 min ÷ 60 = **100,000 hours**
- 1,000,000 views × 15 min ÷ 60 = **250,000 hours**
- 1,000,000 Shorts views × 0.8 min ÷ 60 = **13,333 hours**

### A.3 Revenue arithmetic

`revenue = monthly views ÷ 1,000 × RPM`

Examples:

- 100,000 views × $10 RPM ÷ 1,000 = **$1,000**
- 1,000,000 views × $10 RPM ÷ 1,000 = **$10,000**
- 4,200,000 views × $10 RPM ÷ 1,000 = **$42,000**
- 244,000,000 views × $5 RPM ÷ 1,000 = **$1,220,000**

These are gross, before costs, tax and currency conversion.

## Appendix C — the one-paragraph version

Finance demand is real and large, but it is not evenly distributed. That is not a guarantee. It is a cheap, reversible way to find out whether the audience wants it.
